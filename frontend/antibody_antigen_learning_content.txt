CHAPTER 1: THE BASICS OF IMMUNOLOGY
===================================

1. What is the immune system?

The immune system is the body's defence system. It helps protect us from harmful microorganisms such as viruses, bacteria and fungi.

There are two major parts:
- Innate immunity: the body's first line of defence. It responds quickly and does not target only one specific pathogen.
- Adaptive immunity: a specialised defence system that responds to particular antigens and can develop immunological memory.

Source – NCBI, Immunobiology:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10757/


2. What are antibodies and antigens?

An antigen is a substance that can be recognised by components of the immune system. Proteins on the surface of viruses and bacteria can act as antigens.

An antibody is a specialised protein produced and released by plasma cells. It recognises and binds to a particular antigen.

Important terms:
- Antigen: the target recognised by the immune system.
- Antibody: a protein that recognises and binds to an antigen.
- Epitope: the specific region of an antigen recognised by an antibody.
- Paratope: the antigen-binding region of an antibody.

Remember: An antigen is what is recognised; an antibody is what recognises it.

Source – NCBI, B Cells and Antibodies:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26884/


3. How does our body produce antibodies?

Antibodies are produced by plasma cells, which develop from B lymphocytes (B cells).

The basic process:
1. A B cell recognises an antigen using a receptor on its surface.
2. The B cell receives the signals needed for activation.
3. The activated B cell multiplies.
4. Some resulting cells develop into plasma cells.
5. Plasma cells release antibodies that recognise the same antigen.

Some activated B cells become memory B cells. They help the body respond more quickly if it encounters the same antigen again.

Remember:
- B cells recognise antigens.
- Plasma cells produce and release antibodies.
- Memory B cells contribute to immune memory.

Source – NCBI, B Cells and Antibodies:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26884/


CHAPTER 2: ANTIBODY RECOGNITION AND BINDING
===========================================

4. How does an antibody recognise an antigen?

An antibody recognises an antigen through a specialised binding region. The antibody's binding region interacts with a particular region on the antigen.

The antigen region is called the epitope. The antibody region that binds it is called the paratope.

Recognition depends on:
- Shape compatibility.
- Amino acid composition.
- Charge distribution.
- Chemical properties of the interacting surfaces.

Important: Recognition is not based on shape alone. Chemical properties also help determine whether the two surfaces interact.

Source – Sela-Culang et al. (2013), The Structural Basis of Antibody-Antigen Recognition:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3792396/


5. How does an antibody bind to an antigen?

When an antibody recognises a compatible antigen, the two molecules can bind and form an antibody–antigen complex.

The binding is mainly stabilised by non-covalent molecular interactions:
- Hydrogen bonds: attractions between suitable atoms in molecules.
- Electrostatic interactions: attractions or repulsions between charged groups.
- Van der Waals forces: weak attractions between nearby atoms.
- Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar surfaces associate in ways that reduce their exposure to water.

Binding affinity describes how strongly an antibody binds to an antigenic site. Higher affinity generally means stronger binding.

Sources:
NCBI – B Cells and Antibodies:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26884/

Ramaraj et al. (2012) – Antigen-antibody interface properties:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3443979/


6. What happens after an antibody binds to an antigen?

Antibody binding can help the immune system respond in several ways.

- Neutralisation: antibodies can block viruses or toxins from interacting with or entering cells.
- Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens and make them easier for immune cells to recognise and engulf.
- Complement activation: certain antibodies can activate complement proteins, which help eliminate pathogens.

Remember: Antibodies do not always destroy pathogens directly. They can help other parts of the immune system eliminate them.

Source – NCBI, The Recognition and Effector Mechanisms of Adaptive Immunity:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK27124/


CHAPTER 3: UNDERSTANDING ANTIBODY STRUCTURE
===========================================

7. What is the structure of an antibody?

A typical antibody is a Y-shaped protein made up of four protein chains:
- Two identical heavy chains.
- Two identical light chains.

The chains are connected by disulfide bonds and other interactions.

Important parts:
- Fab region: the two arms of the antibody. They contain the antigen-binding sites.
- Fc region: the stem of the antibody. It interacts with other components of the immune system.
- Variable regions: regions that differ between antibodies and contribute to antigen recognition.
- Constant regions: regions that are more similar within an antibody class and help determine antibody functions.

Both heavy and light chains contribute to the antigen-binding sites.

Source – NCBI, B Cells and Antibodies:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26884/


8. What are CDR regions?

CDR stands for Complementarity-Determining Region. CDRs are variable loops found in the antigen-binding regions of antibodies.

Each typical antibody has six CDR loops:
- Heavy chain: CDR-H1, CDR-H2 and CDR-H3.
- Light chain: CDR-L1, CDR-L2 and CDR-L3.

Why are CDRs important?
- They form a major part of the antigen-binding surface.
- Their amino acid sequences vary between antibodies.
- They help determine which antigens an antibody can recognise.
- They contribute to the specificity of antibody–antigen interactions.

Why is CDR-H3 important?
CDR-H3 is often particularly important because it is highly variable and can make significant contributions to antigen recognition. However, all six CDRs can contribute to binding.

Connection to structural analysis:
Researchers study CDRs because differences in their amino acid sequences can change the antibody's binding surface and influence antigen recognition.

Peng et al. (2022) – Antibody CDR amino acids underlying recognition of diverse protein antigens:
